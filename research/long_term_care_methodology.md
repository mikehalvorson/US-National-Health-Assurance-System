# Long-Term Care: sources and methodology

Evidence base for the Long-Term Care tab. Every figure used on the page traces
to a source here with a confidence grade. Dollar figures are stated at the scale
of their source year; the fiscal model's net-new LTC cost is carried in real
2023 dollars and displayed at 2024 scale like every other parameter.

## 1. Why the current U.S. system fails

- **Medicare does not cover long-term custodial care.** Medicare pays only
  limited skilled care (up to 100 days per benefit period after a qualifying
  hospital stay), not ongoing help with bathing, dressing, eating, and
  supervision. This is the most common and most costly misunderstanding in
  American retirement planning. Confidence: high.
  Source: Medicaid.gov Long-Term Services & Supports; CMS Medicare coverage of
  skilled nursing facility care.

- **Medicaid is the default payer, but only after impoverishment.** Medicaid
  paid 61% of the $415B spent on long-term services and supports (LTSS) in 2022
  ($284B home and community-based, $131B institutional). Eligibility for
  long-term care generally requires spending assets down to roughly $2,000 for
  an individual in most states. Out-of-pocket paid 17%. Confidence: high.
  Source: Mathematica / Medicaid.gov "Medicaid LTSS Users and Expenditures by
  Service Category, 2022"; KFF "10 Things About LTSS."

- **Costs are catastrophic for families.** 2024 median annual costs (Genworth /
  CareScout Cost of Care Survey): private nursing-home room $127,750; in-home
  home health aide $77,792; assisted living in the low-to-mid $60,000s.
  Confidence: high. Source: Genworth/CareScout 2024 Cost of Care Survey.

- **Lifetime risk is the rule, not the exception.** About 70% of adults who
  reach 65 will develop severe LTSS needs before they die; women average 3.7
  years of need, men 2.2 years; about one in seven will spend more than two
  years in a nursing home. Confidence: high. Source: HHS ASPE, "What Is the
  Lifetime Risk of Needing and Receiving LTSS?" and Risks and Financing 2022.

- **Unmet need is measured.** About 711,000 people sat on Medicaid home- and
  community-based services (HCBS) waiting lists in 2024 (up from 692,000),
  waiting an average of roughly 40 months. Confidence: high. Source: KFF, "A
  Look at Waiting Lists for Medicaid HCBS."

- **Families absorb the gap unpaid.** About 38 million family caregivers
  provided roughly 36 billion hours of unpaid care in 2021, an estimated
  economic value of about $600B, more than total U.S. out-of-pocket health
  spending that year. Confidence: high. Source: AARP, "Valuing the Invaluable,
  2023 Update."

- **The workforce is underpaid and short.** The direct-care workforce (home
  care aides, residential aides, nursing assistants) reached about 5.4 million
  in 2024; the median wage was $17.36/hr and median annual earnings were just
  under $26,000; home-care turnover ran near 75%. PHI projects about 9.7 million
  total direct-care job openings from 2024 to 2034. Confidence: high. Source:
  PHI, "Direct Care Workers in the United States: Key Facts 2024/2025."

## 2. Systems in other countries that work

- **Germany (statutory long-term care insurance, Pflegeversicherung, 1995).**
  Mandatory social insurance attached to health insurance; covers every
  resident regardless of age. 2024 contribution 3.4% of gross wages split
  employer/employee, with a childless surcharge (about 4.0% total). Pays graded
  benefits by five care levels and, distinctively, a cash allowance
  (Pflegegeld) that family caregivers can take instead of, or blended with,
  in-kind services. Deliberately a partial ("Teilkasko") benefit: it caps the
  public share rather than covering every cost. Confidence: high (design),
  medium (exact 2024 rate). Source: EURAXESS Germany LTC guide; German Federal
  Ministry of Health.

- **Japan (Kaigo Hoken, long-term care insurance, 2000).** Mandatory insurance;
  everyone 40+ pays income-scaled premiums, benefits from 65+ (or 40-64 for
  aging-related conditions). Funded roughly half premiums, half taxes; users pay
  a 10% copay (20-30% for higher incomes). Municipal certification of care need,
  care managers, and a deliberate tilt toward home and community-based care.
  Widely credited with "socializing" care and relieving family burden.
  Confidence: high. Source: Commonwealth Fund Japan profile; Japan Health Policy
  NOW; Japan Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

- **Netherlands (Wlz long-term care act, 2015; AWBZ from 1968).** Universal,
  income-related contributions fund the most intensive 24/7 care (Wlz), with
  municipal social support (Wmo) and insured district nursing (Zvw) covering
  lighter needs. Among the highest LTC spenders in the OECD (~4.4% of GDP). Home
  care is anchored by the Buurtzorg model: small self-managing neighborhood
  nursing teams (about 10 nurses per ~10,000 residents, minimal management),
  associated with fewer hospitalizations, fewer institutional placements, high
  patient and staff satisfaction, and lower cost per client. Confidence: high
  (spending, model), medium (cost-savings magnitude). Source: OECD Health at a
  Glance 2023; Commonwealth Fund and Urban Institute Buurtzorg case studies.

- **Nordic tax-funded municipal care (Denmark, Sweden, Norway).** No separate
  insurance: general taxation funds universal, municipally run elder care.
  Denmark pioneered reablement (short, goal-oriented rehabilitation so people
  regain independence rather than receive indefinite help; required of all
  municipalities since 2015) and mandatory preventive home visits for older
  residents. Nordic public LTC spending is the highest in the OECD (Norway 3.5%,
  Sweden 3.4%, Denmark 3.2% of GDP). Confidence: high. Source: OECD Health at a
  Glance 2023; Healthcare Denmark; peer-reviewed reablement evaluations.

- **Comparative spending anchor (one source for every bar).** All eight bars in
  the GDP chart use OECD Health at a Glance 2023, total long-term care as a share
  of GDP, 2021: Netherlands 4.4%, Norway 3.5%, Sweden 3.4%, Denmark 3.2%, Germany
  2.5%, Japan 2.2%, OECD average 1.8%, United States 1.3%. This replaces an
  earlier mix that had Germany at 2.1%, Japan at 2.0%, and a public-only U.S.
  figure of 1.0%; the single-series values above are what HaG 2023 reports and
  are internally consistent. Countries that cover the benefit universally cluster
  at 2.2-4.4%; the United States commits the smallest share and rations by
  impoverishment and waiting lists. Confidence: high. Source: OECD Health at a
  Glance 2023, "Long-term care spending and unit costs"; cross-checked against
  the A-Mark Foundation's HaG 2023 digest.

- **Dollars per person (the second reading in each tooltip).** Long-term care
  spending per person is derived transparently as (share of GDP) x (GDP per
  capita, USD PPP, 2021, World Bank NY.GDP.PCAP.PP.CD): Norway ~$3,227,
  Netherlands ~$3,017, Denmark ~$2,218, Sweden ~$2,114, Germany ~$1,566, Japan
  ~$1,012, United States ~$929, OECD average ~$922. This is the same spending
  divided by population, so it is a same-unit (per-person) comparison, not a
  cross-scale one. The point it makes: in raw price-adjusted dollars the U.S.
  sits near the OECD average, because the U.S. economy is large; the failure is
  that the money is means-tested and rationed, and the per-capita figure still
  omits the ~$600B in unpaid family care. Confidence: high (arithmetic on two
  high-confidence series). Source: OECD HaG 2023 (shares); World Bank (GDP per
  capita, PPP, 2021).

Common threads in the systems that work: coverage is universal and automatic;
financing is a predictable dedicated stream, not a means-tested trapdoor; the
benefit is deliberately home-first; family caregivers are paid or supported
rather than assumed free; and one accountable public body plans capacity.

## 3. The plan and its cost inside the framework

- **The benefit already sits in the fiscal model.** Long-term care is carried as
  the `ltcExpansion` parameter (low $150B, mode $230B, high $330B per year in
  real 2023 dollars, ~$236B at 2024 scale), inside the Expansions group summed
  into total NHA cost (`model.ts`, `cExpansions`). It is the framework's single
  largest benefit expansion and is not double-counted with the existing $415B of
  LTSS spending; it represents the *net new* national spending a universal,
  home-first benefit adds above today's baseline. Anchor: CBO comprehensive-LTSS
  option (+~4.4% of NHE). Confidence: medium.

- **The governing body already exists.** The plan assigns long-term care to the
  National Long-Term Care Authority (NLTCA) under the Administration for Care
  Delivery and Regional Health, with a 70% home-and-community service target
  (`gov.ts`). Confidence: high (design).

- **Workforce assessment (three honest headcounts).** The binding constraint is
  the direct-care workforce (aides), not physicians. The staffing figure is
  presented as three headcounts of workers, all in the same unit so the bars
  compare like with like:
  - **Today (2024): ~5.4M** direct-care workers (PHI 2025). High.
  - **Current system by 2034: ~6.2M.** PHI 2025 projects ~772,000 NEW direct-care
    jobs over 2024-2034; 5.4M + 0.772M = ~6.2M is the employment level the
    existing system needs simply to keep pace with aging. High.
  - **Universal benefit at maturity: ~7.5M.** A planning estimate: the plan's
    ~5.0M covered full-time-equivalent (FTE) aides divided by a ~0.67 full-time
    fraction (direct care is heavily part-time) is ~7.5M workers. It exceeds the
    2034 baseline because the benefit also serves people now rationed out (the
    ~711k on waiting lists and broader unmet need) and expands paid home care
    under the 70% home-first target. Confidence: low (the FTE count and part-time
    fraction are planning assumptions).

  Separately, PHI's **~9.7M "total openings" 2024-2034** is a flow (new jobs plus
  all replacement hires as workers churn out at ~75% home-care turnover), not a
  point-in-time headcount; it is quoted in prose and never drawn as a bar next to
  the stock figures. The 2034 horizon is PHI/BLS's ten-year projection window and
  also ~8 years into a 2026 start. The Workforce tab sizes this as its own block
  (`LTC_WORKFORCE` in `workforce.ts`), kept out of the insurance-transition
  ledger (whose entrant-pace math must not absorb millions of aides). The merit
  immigration pathway (NHTIB shortage lists, HCRB credential recognition) now
  lists direct-care roles alongside clinicians. Confidence: medium overall.

- **Aide wage-floor cost (net new, and separate from `ltcExpansion`).** The base
  LTC benefit prices care at prevailing wages; a wage floor above the $17.36/hr
  2024 median is additional. Derivation, carried in the model as
  `params.ltcWageFloor` and mirrored by `ltcWageFloorCost()`:
  covered direct-care workforce ~5.0M FTE (range 4-6M) x 2,080 hr x a loaded
  ~$5.00/hr lift toward a ~$22/hr living-wage floor (range $3.50-7.50) =
  about $52B/yr in 2023 dollars (range ~$29B-94B), roughly $53B at 2024 scale.
  This is what raises the headline framework cost when aide pay is factored in.
  Confidence: low (the covered-FTE count and the target floor are planning
  assumptions).
